A ledger nobody can quietly rewrite.

Blockchain is a shared record book, copied across thousands of computers and sealed with maths, so nobody has to trust a bank, a company or each other to agree on what happened. This guide explains it from scratch. Every idea comes with something you can poke at.

This is its hash, a fingerprint of exactly what you typed. Change a single letter and the whole fingerprint changes. Most of blockchain is built on this one trick.
Ten short chapters below. Take about 20 minutes, or jump around with the numbers on the left.
1

It took thirty years of failed attempts

Bitcoin didn't appear out of nowhere. Cryptographers had been trying to make digital money and tamper-proof records since the early 80s. Each attempt solved one piece of the puzzle. Step through the timeline to see how the pieces came together.

The big problem was double-spending. A digital file can be copied, so how do you stop someone spending the same coin twice without a bank keeping the books? Bitcoin's answer was to make everyone keep the books together.

2

Hashes: a fingerprint for any piece of data

A hash function takes any input, from one word to a whole film, and squeezes it into a fixed-size fingerprint. Bitcoin uses one called SHA-256, which always gives 64 characters.

It has three properties that matter: the same input always gives the same hash, a tiny change gives a completely different hash, and you can't work backwards from a hash to the input.

How many of the 256 bits are different

Each square above is one bit of the fingerprint. Red squares are bits that flipped. Change one character and about half of them flip. That's called the avalanche effect, and it's why nobody can tweak data without the fingerprint giving it away.

3

A block is a page of the ledger, and sealing it takes work

Transactions are grouped into blocks, like pages in a ledger. To add a page, the network sets a puzzle: find a number (the nonce) that makes the block's hash start with a certain number of zeros.

There's no clever shortcut. You just keep guessing. That guessing is mining, and the difficulty slider controls how many zeros are needed. Each extra zero makes the puzzle about 16 times harder.

Try mining, then edit a single letter of the data. The seal breaks straight away because the hash has changed. Real Bitcoin needs about 19 leading zeros, and the whole network makes roughly 1021 guesses every second.

4

Each page holds the fingerprint of the page before it

This is what makes it a chain. Every block includes the previous block's hash. If you change an old block, its hash changes, so the next block's "previous hash" no longer matches, and the break ripples all the way to the end.

Rewriting one block means redoing the work for all the blocks after it, while the rest of the network keeps adding new ones. On Bitcoin, rewriting even an hour of history would take more computing power than everyone else combined.

5

Thousands of people keep their own copy

There's no master copy on a central server. Every participant, called a node, keeps the full ledger and checks every new block for themselves. If someone tampers with their copy, it simply stops matching everyone else's.

Tamper with a node below, even re-mining it so it looks valid on its own, then ask the network to compare.

To fake the record, an attacker would have to change more than half of all copies at the same moment. Bitcoin has more than 15,000 publicly reachable nodes, spread around the world.

6

Your key is your identity, and your signature proves it

There are no usernames or passwords. You have a key pair: a private key you never share, and a public key that works like your account number. Signing a transaction with your private key proves it came from you, and anyone can check that with your public key.

These are real cryptographic keys generated in your browser right now, using the same family of maths (elliptic curves) that Bitcoin and Ethereum use.

Whoever holds the private key controls the coins. That's why people say "not your keys, not your coins". Lose the key and nobody can reset it for you.

7

Thousands of transactions, one fingerprint

A block can hold thousands of transactions. Instead of hashing them all in one lump, they're hashed in pairs, then those hashes are paired and hashed again, until one hash is left: the Merkle root. It goes in the block header.

This means a phone wallet can prove one transaction is in a block using just a few hashes, without downloading the whole thing. Edit a transaction to see which hashes change, or click "Prove" to see the minimum proof.

8

Who gets to write the next page?

Someone has to pick which block comes next. The rule for choosing is the consensus mechanism. The two big ones are:

Proof of Work (Bitcoin): miners race to solve the hashing puzzle. More computing power means more wins, and burns more electricity. Proof of Stake (Ethereum since 2022): validators lock up coins as a deposit. The protocol picks the next block-writer at random, weighted by stake, and cheaters lose their deposit.

Both systems face the same weak spot: anyone with more than half the power or stake could rewrite recent history. That's the 51% attack. Drag one slider past half to see it. When Ethereum switched to Proof of Stake in 2022, its energy use fell by about 99.95%.

9

Programs that hold money and can't be argued with

Ethereum added a big idea in 2015: put small programs on the chain, called smart contracts. They hold money and follow their code exactly. No company runs them, and nobody can change the rules once they're deployed.

10
Where blockchain is heading now

  

    
The biggest problem of the last decade was scaling. Ethereum's main chain handles only about 15–20 transactions a second, and fees spiked when it got busy. The main fix is layer 2 rollups: networks that bundle hundreds of transactions off-chain and post one compressed batch to the main chain, so everyone shares the cost.
